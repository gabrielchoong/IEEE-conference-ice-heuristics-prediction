\begin{abstract}
	Road transportation remains a primary contributor to global greenhouse gas emissions, where
	thermodynamic limits inherent to Internal Combustion Engines (ICE) constrain efficiency and
	complicate manual engine calibration. Although artificial neural networks can map non-linear
	engine emissions, manual architecture design remains a costly trial-and-error process prone
	to severe overfitting. In this study, we propose a hybrid Artificial Neural Network and Harris
	Hawks Optimization (ANN-HHO) framework for multi-target vehicle emission modeling, predicting
	mathematically estimated $\ce{NO_x}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ from OBD-II vehicle telemetry. The proposed
	framework simultaneously optimizes network topology and training hyperparameters under both
	unrestricted and compute-constrained regimes. Empirical results demonstrate that the proposed
	unrestricted model (HHO-UR) achieves superior predictive fidelity with an $R^2$ of $0.9823$ for
	$\ce{NO_x}$ and $0.9950$ for $\ce{CO2}$, decisively resolving the generalization failure observed
	in the manual baseline model ($R^2 = 0.5714 \pm 0.2240$). Furthermore, under a restricted
	budget ($\le 20$ epochs), restricted HHO (HHO-R) attains an $88.7\%$ reduction in training epochs
	(17 vs. 150) with an $R^2$ of $0.9327$, cutting search wall-clock duration from 9.1 hours to 76
	minutes. Comparative evaluation against alternative metaheuristics (PSO, ACOR, and SCA) confirms
	that the HHO framework consistently discovers compact, generalizable two-layer topologies,
	demonstrating a scalable and computationally efficient methodology for intelligent automotive engine management.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The Internal Combustion Engine is the backbone of modern society. Rapid adoption of passenger vehicles throughout the
20\textsuperscript{th} and 21\textsuperscript{st} century uncovered the fundamental downside of the combustion process.
The chemical reaction of igniting fossil fuels in the presence of air under high temperature and high pressure releases
significant quantities of greenhouse gases responsible for global warming such as nitrogen oxides ($\ce{NO_x}$), carbon
dioxide ($\ce{CO2}$), carbon monoxide ($\ce{CO}$) and sulphur dioxide ($\ce{SO2}$).

The effectiveness of manual calibration of ICE systems is inherently limited by the thermodynamics of the combustion
process \cite{weteringArtificialNeuralNetworkBased2023}. An ideal optimization objective in engine management is the
simultaneous minimization of both $\ce{NO_x}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ emissions resulting from hydrocarbon combustion. However,
these two target pollutants exhibit a fundamental thermodynamic Pareto trade-off governed by in-cylinder operating
temperatures. Higher combustion temperatures promote more complete fuel oxidation, thereby maximizing thermal efficiency
and reducing specific fuel consumption alongside $\ce{CO2}$ emissions. Conversely, these elevated temperatures provide
the activation energy required for the thermal fixation of atmospheric nitrogen into nitrogen oxides ($\ce{NO_x}$) via
the Zeldovich mechanism, while accelerating the thermal decomposition of nitrogen dioxide ($\ce{NO2}$) into toxic
nitrogen monoxide ($\ce{NO}$) \cite{nitrogenDioxideThermalDecomposition}. This opposing physical relationship makes
concurrent emission reduction highly non-linear and difficult to navigate through traditional heuristics.

Manual calibration of the thermodynamics of an IC Engine remain costly and time consuming. Machine learning (ML) methods
such as artificial neural networks (ANN) offer a powerful alternative to map out the relationships of several crucial
factors of the system. The modern ICE system generates complex and non-linear telemetry data via the standardized
On-Board Diagnostics II (OBD-II) architecture. Since ANNs excel at mapping cross-domain, multi-dimentional, non-linear
relationships\cite{modelingNonlinearRelationships}, they are well suited to model the emission outputs.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section II reviews the existing literature; Section III details the
proposed methodology; Section IV compares the results of the proposed methodology with current approaches; Section V
concludes the findings of this paper.

\section{Related Works}

Recent efforts in ICE optimization for lowering emissions leveraging ANNs have proven to reduce developmental time and
costs \cite{bhattApplicationArtificialNeural2022, tuanhoangReviewApplicationArtificial2021} due to the adaptability and
flexibility of these neural networks \cite{karunamurthyPredictionICEngine2023,
inezahavugimanaReviewArtificialIntelligent2023}. Several studies confirmed the reliability of pairing an ANN with
optimization algorithms to design a mathematically optimal network architecture
\cite{sharmaApplicationMachineLearning2023, usluOptimizationDieselEngine2020, singhSampleddataModelValidation2020}. The
application of optimization algorithms on ANNs demonstrated a better performance overall for training a model on IC
Engine telemetry \cite{gunawanApplicationArtificialNeural2024} when compared to manual-tuned ANNs.

Metaheuristic optimization techniques, particularly nature-inspired algorithms such as the Harris Hawks Optimization
(HHO) have proven to be superior in engineering design \cite{heidariHarrisHawksOptimization2019}. It utilizes a smart
search strategy mirroring the pack hunting of Harris Hawks to find a near optimal approximation solution. Furthermore,
recent studies noted the application of machine learning based low level control for the Engine Control Unit (ECU)
\cite{omranDeepReinforcementLearning2024}. Together, these studies highlight promising methodologies to be tested and
applied to ICE system optimizations.

\section{Proposed Methodology}

\subsection{Nitrogen Oxides $\ce{NO_x}$ Approximation}\label{sec:nox-modelling}

Nitrogen Oxides ($\ce{NO_x}$) are notorious for being difficult to predict in a combustion process using pure
mathematical models. The common way of predicting $\ce{NO_x}$ is to use a supervised-learning algorithm to achieve
relatively high accuracy. Nitrogen Oxide ($\ce{NO}$) has a formation rate \cite{THOMPSON1973787} of

\begin{equation}
	\frac{d[NO]}{dt} = 1.503 \times 10 \cdot T^{-0.5} \cdot [O_2]^{0.5}
	\cdot [N_2] \cdot \frac{[O]}{[O]\textsubscript{eq}} \cdot e^{\frac{-134.7}{RT}}
	\label{eqn:no-rate}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $T$ is temperature [K],
	\item $[O]\textsubscript{eq}$ equilibrium atomic oxygen concentration, and
	\item $R$ is the ideal gas constant [J/mol$ \cdot $K] .
\end{itemize}
From \eqref{eqn:no-rate},
\begin{equation}
	\ce{NO_x} = \int_{0}^{t} f(T, [\ce{O_2}], [\ce{N_2}], [O], [O]\textsubscript{eq}) \quad dt
	\label{eqn:nox-integral}
\end{equation}

Under high temperatures, thermal equilibrium favours the formation of $\ce{NO}$ due to the thermal decomposition of
nitrogen dioxide ($\ce{NO_2}$), which initiates from \SI{150}{\degreeCelsius} at atmospheric pressure
\cite{nitrogenDioxideThermalDecomposition}:

\begin{equation}
	\ce{2NO2(g) ->[\SI{150}{\degreeCelsius}][1 a.t.m] 2NO(g) + O2(g)}\label{eq:no2tono}
\end{equation}

When exhaust gases escape the combustion chamber into lower-temperature exhaust manifolds, thermal energy is released
and the chemical equilibrium shifts back towards $\ce{NO_2}$. In this modeling framework, the collective mixture of
these oxides is denoted by $\ce{NO_x}$ in \eqref{eqn:nox-integral}.

To achieve a suitable approximation, $\ce{NO_x}$ emissions are approximated using the equation below

\begin{equation}
	NO_x{est} = a \cdot \text{AFR}^b \cdot T_{intake}^c \cdot
	\text{RPM}^d \cdot e^{\frac{-E_a}{RT}}. \label{eqn:nox-estimate}
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eqn:nox-estimate} serves as an estimation for the actual $\ce{NO_x}$ emission values,
where coefficients $a,b,c,d$ are tunable during regression. This equation simplifies \eqref{eqn:nox-integral}
where $[\ce{N_2}], [\ce{O_2}], [\ce{O}], [\ce{O}]\textsubscript{eq}$ are related to AFR and RPM since AFR
is the ratio between air and fuel, where air is composed of mostly Nitrogen and Oxygen. The intake temperature
$T_{intake}$ remains from the previous equation, and $E_a$ can be assumed as 134.7.

\subsection{Carbon Dioxide $\ce{CO2}$ Approximation}\label{sec:co(-_2-)-modelling}

$\ce{CO2}$ closely relates to the Vehicle Specific Power (VSP) in IC Engines. The formula of VSP is given by

\begin{equation}
	VSP = v(1.1a + 9.18grade + 0.132) + 0.000302v^3 \label{eq:vsp}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $v$ is the vehicle speed [m/s],
	\item $a$ is the vehicle acceleration [m/s\textsuperscript{2}], and
	\item $grade$ is the landform slope [-].
\end{itemize}

$\ce{CO2}$ can be approximated with VSP values by \eqref{eqn:co2_and_vsp} \cite{CHEN2024133892}.

\begin{equation}
	f(VSP) = 0.00995 \cdot VSP^2 + 0.2573 \cdot VSP + 1.443 \label{eqn:co2_and_vsp}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Proposed ANN-Metaheuristic Framework}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
	\caption{Proposed Metaheuristic Neural Architecture and Hyperparameter Optimization}
	\label{alg:metaheuristic_optimization}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\REQUIRE Training set $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$, validation set $\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$,
		population size $N_p = 15$, search iterations $T_{\max} = 5$, metaheuristic operator $\mathcal{M}
		\in \{\text{ACOR}, \text{PSO}, \text{HHO}, \text{SCA}\}$, parameter bounds $\mathbf{\Omega}$.
		\ENSURE Optimal configuration $\mathbf{x}^*$, evaluated surrogate model $\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{x}^*}$.

		\STATE \textbf{Initialize:} Population $\mathbf{P}^{(0)} = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots,
		\mathbf{x}_{N_p}\} \sim \mathcal{U}(\mathbf{\Omega})$, global minimum fitness $f^* \leftarrow \infty$.

		\FOR{iteration $t = 1$ \TO $T_{\max}$}
		\FOR{each candidate solution $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbf{P}^{(t-1)}$}
		\STATE Decode $\mathbf{x}_i \to \{L, [u_1, \dots, u_L], \sigma, \mathcal{O}, E_{\text{proxy}}, B\}$.
		\STATE Instantiate FNN $\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{x}_i}$ with $L$ layers, hidden units $u_l$, and activation $\sigma$.
		\STATE Configure optimizer $\mathcal{O}$ with learning rate $\eta = 0.001$.
		\STATE Partition $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ into mini-batches of size $B$.

		\FOR{epoch $e = 1$ \TO $E_{\text{proxy}}$}
		\FOR{each mini-batch $(\mathbf{X}_b, \mathbf{y}_b) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$}
		\STATE $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_b \leftarrow \mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{x}_i}(\mathbf{X}_b)$
		\STATE $\mathcal{L} \leftarrow \frac{1}{|\mathbf{X}_b|} \|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_b - \mathbf{y}_b\|_2^2$ \COMMENT{MSE Loss}
		\STATE $\mathbf{W} \leftarrow \mathbf{W} - \eta \nabla_{\mathbf{W}} \mathcal{L}$
		\ENDFOR
		\ENDFOR

		\STATE Forward validation pass: $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{val}} \leftarrow
		\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{x}_i}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{val}})$.
		\STATE Compute coefficient of determination: \\
		\quad $R^2 \leftarrow 1 - \frac{\sum_{j} (\mathbf{y}_{\text{val}, j} -
		\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{val}, j})^2}{\sum_{j} (\mathbf{y}_{\text{val}, j} - \bar{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{val}})^2}$.
		\STATE Assign fitness: $f(\mathbf{x}_i) \leftarrow 1 - R^2$.

		\IF{$f(\mathbf{x}_i) < f^*$}
		\STATE $f^* \leftarrow f(\mathbf{x}_i)$, \quad $\mathbf{x}^* \leftarrow \mathbf{x}_i$.
		\ENDIF
		\ENDFOR
		\STATE Update population positions: $\mathbf{P}^{(t)} \leftarrow \mathcal{M}(\mathbf{P}^{(t-1)}, \mathbf{x}^*)$.
		\ENDFOR

		\STATE \textbf{Stage-2 Evaluation:} Evaluate $\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{x}^*}$ on held-out test
		telemetry $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$ to record generalization metrics.
		\RETURN $\mathbf{x}^*, \mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{x}^*}$.
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The proposed optimization framework operates on a Two-Stage Neural Architecture Search (NAS)
paradigm, formalized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:metaheuristic_optimization}. Rather than evaluating
arbitrarily deep network topologies over exhaustive training cycles, the continuous search vector
$\mathbf{x} \in \mathbf{\Omega}$ dynamically parameterizes both network capacity (number of layers
	$L \in [1, 5]$, unit width per layer $u_l \in [2, 256]$, and activation $\sigma \in \{\text{ReLU},
\text{Sigmoid}\}$) and the gradient descent dynamics (optimizer $\mathcal{O}$, batch size $B \in
[16, 64]$, and proxy epoch budget $E_{\text{proxy}} \le 20$).

During Stage-1 search, each candidate agent is trained under the restricted proxy budget using
standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss. The resulting validation performance is mapped to a
minimization objective $f(\mathbf{x}) = 1 - R^2$. Population positions are then updated via the
respective metaheuristic operator $\mathcal{M}$ (ACOR, AIW-PSO, Original HHO, or Dev-SCA). The metaheuristic
algorithms and candidate parameter spaces were implemented using the open-source MEALPY
framework~\cite{van2023mealpy,van2023groundwater,ahmed2021comprehensive}. Once the
population converges, Stage-2 extracts the global optimal parameter set $\mathbf{x}^*$ and
benchmarks test generalization against the manual baseline model.

\subsection{Architectural Convergence and Overfitting Analysis}

Despite the unbounded search space permitting topologies between 1
and 5 hidden layers, all four restricted algorithms independently converged on shallow, wide
configurations consisting of exactly two hidden layers ($[205, 256]$ for ACOR, $[242, 125]$ for
PSO, $[138, 256]$ for HHO, and $[94, 202]$ for SCA). The widened first hidden layer ($> 94$ units,
expanding up to 242) provides an expressive initial non-linear projection for the raw telemetry
features, while avoiding the gradient degradation and slow propagation typical of deeper
architectures under restricted training horizons.

This architectural property directly explains the failure mode of the manual baseline model. As
depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:loss_convergence_comparison}(b), the manual baseline's training loss
plummets from $18,000$ to $52.1 \pm 14.8$ (reaching as low as $31.8$ at Epoch 150). However, this
apparent convergence is a pathology of premature learning rate decay. The baseline utilized a
StepLR scheduler that decayed the learning rate by a factor of 10 at Epoch 30 and again at Epoch
60. These step drops artificially suppressed the training error by trapping the network weights in
a narrow local basin of the loss landscape, halting gradient-driven exploration. Consequently, the
network memorised training noise, yielding an artificially suppressed training MSE while failing to
generalise to the non-linear thermal formation kinetics of $\ce{NO_x}$ on unseen telemetry (where
test loss stalled at $97.12 \pm 25.01$ and $R^2$ collapsed to $0.5714$).

In contrast, the metaheuristically discovered models employed adaptive, flat optimisers (Adam,
Nadam, RMSprop) without aggressive scheduler decay, coupled with wider hidden layer
representations. This allowed the models to escape shallow local extrema and converge toward
flatter, generalisable minima, securing test $R^2$ scores above $0.93$ for $\ce{NO_x}$ and $0.99$
for $\ce{CO2}$ in under 20 epochs.

\subsection{Discussion}

The performance of the two models on the target variables were evaluated using equations obtained in
Sections~\ref{sec:co(-_2-)-modelling} and~\ref{sec:nox-modelling}. The ANN-HHO model demonstrated effective
hyperparameter optimisation, resulting in a high performance regression model. It is important to note that the dataset
used in this study is based on open-source OBD-II vehicle telemetry data~\cite{cephasax_obdii_ds3}
and does not include actual sensor readings for either
$ NO_x $ or $ CO_2 $. Instead, both target variables were derived using mathematical models applied to OBD-II data.
The observed performance improvements might vary accordingly on the nature and quality of the data being used. The lack
of direct sensor measurements introduces a key limitation in the validity of the regression results. While modelled
estimates provide a viable approximation, they inherently lack the resolution and accuracy of real-world sensor outputs.
For $ NO_x $ in particular, the absence of an actual sensor exacerbates issues related to skewed data distributions
and the presence of outliers. Integrating genuine $ NO_x $ sensors into the data collection pipeline would greatly
improve the fidelity of the dataset, enabling more reliable training and evaluation of machine learning models.
Moreover, certain data fields within the original dataset may be skewed, potentially contributing to underfitting in the
$ NO_x $ predictions. Furthermore, equation~\eqref{eqn:nox-estimate} was derived from the formation rate of Nitrogen
Oxides $ NO_x $, which may introduce inaccuracies for representing the actual emissions data for $ NO_x $. Despite
the limitations, the experimental approach presented in this experiment can be adapted to other domains requiring
predictive modelling and optimisation based training strategies.

The instability observed in the $ R^2 $ scores for $ NO_x $ across different experiments can be primarily attributed
to the statistical characteristics of the original dataset. Specifically, the distribution of the $ NO_x $ variable is
highly skewed and contains a significant number of extreme outliers. This leads to inconsistent performance of the
regression models, particularly when the evaluation metric is sensitive to deviations from the central tendency of the
data. A closer inspection of the dataset reveals that the values for $ NO_x $ are not symmetrically distributed;
rather, they are heavily concentrated around lower magnitudes, while a few samples exhibit abnormally high emission
levels. These outliers disproportionately influence the training process and evaluation metrics, often distorting the
perceived performance of the model even when the underlying trend is correctly captured. The R-squared score, while
useful in measuring goodness-of-fit, becomes less reliable under such conditions, making it difficult to fairly assess
model performance across different experimental runs. Importantly, the issues stemming from the data distribution are
not a consequence of the modelling methodology. Any attempt to normalize or transform the $ NO_x $ variable was found
to degrade the statistical coherence of the dataset, as the variable is already close to being centered but still
maintains a mean greater than zero. Further transformations would risk distorting the emission pattern or weakening the
relationships encoded within the original feature set. Given these constraints, it was determined that retaining the raw
distribution of $ NO_x $ was preferable to introducing artificial biases through aggressive preprocessing.

Therefore, the variations in $ R^2 $ scores for $ NO_x $ should be interpreted with caution. While the ANN-HHO model
still demonstrates strong performance overall, the presence of a non-ideal data distribution highlights the limitations
inherent to open-source datasets. These findings reinforce the importance of robust data collection procedures and the
potential impact of data quality on model evaluation, particularly in environmental prediction tasks involving naturally
imbalanced phenomena like pollutant emissions.

A similar observation can be made regarding the evaluation of $ CO_2 $ predictions. Although the model demonstrated a
significant performance gain in terms of $ R^2 $ and reduction in loss, these improvements must also be considered
within the context of how the target variable was derived. Since $ CO_2 $ values were generated from an estimation
equation rather than obtained through direct measurement, the accuracy of the regression is inherently constrained by
the fidelity of the underlying model used for generation. Moreover, estimation errors embedded in the original equation
may have introduced subtle patterns in the dataset that the model was able to exploit, leading to artificially high
performance indicators. This further reinforces the importance of using ground truth sensor data to validate results and
confirm generalization capabilities. Despite this, the ANN-HHO model's consistent improvements over the baseline ANN
across multiple runs support its reliability as an optimisation-based approach. However, these findings should be
interpreted with the understanding that both $ CO_2 $ and $ NO_x $ values are, at their core, theoretical
approximations. Future work involving actual sensor data would allow for a more comprehensive validation of the methods
applied in this study and help eliminate ambiguities caused by the limitations of synthetic target generation.

\section{Conclusion and Future Works}

This study demonstrated that pairing artificial neural networks with metaheuristic optimization
(ANN-HHO) delivers high-fidelity surrogate modeling for internal combustion engine emissions while
eliminating manual trial-and-error bottlenecks. Under compute-restricted proxy evaluations ($\le 20$
epochs), metaheuristically discovered architectures converged on robust, shallow-wide topologies (two
layers, $\ge 138$ units), decisively preventing the scheduler-induced overfitting that degraded the
manual baseline ($R^2 = 0.5714 \pm 0.2240$). Specifically, HHO-UR attained top $\ce{NO_x}$ fidelity
($R^2 = 0.9823$), while HHO-R achieved an $88.7\%$ reduction in training epochs (17 versus 150) and
reduced search runtime down to 76 minutes. ACOR and AIW-PSO further validated the Pareto efficiency of
the continuous metaheuristic search space.

Future research will focus on deploying the ANN-HHO surrogate onto physical Engine Control Unit (ECU)
hardware to evaluate real-time inference latency and closed-loop control dynamics. In addition,
integrating physical transient $\ce{NO_x}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ sensor measurements will replace proxy
telemetry equations, enabling direct calibration across diverse vehicle powertrains.
